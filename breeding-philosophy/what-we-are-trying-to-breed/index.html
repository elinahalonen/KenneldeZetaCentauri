Breeding Philosophy

What We Are Trying to Breed

The goal is not the “best dog” in the abstract. It is to stack the odds towards dogs with bodies and minds that allow them to live rich, capable and satisfying lives as the kind of animals they actually are.

Planned coverage (draft notes — not final copy):

  • Centre the dog's lived experience: what is it like to be this dog, in this body, with this nervous system, these motivations and relationships?
  • Health and functional soundness as lived physical experience, not simply test status or appearance.
  • Temperament: curiosity, persistence, environmental confidence, recovery, emotional regulation, useful sensitivity, cooperative agency.
  • Working motivation because it gives the dog access to intrinsically meaningful activities, not because titles are the goal.
  • Functional benchmark: could this dog physically and temperamentally do the job the breed was developed to do?
  • Agency and competence: dogs should have meaningful opportunities to choose, investigate, solve problems and affect outcomes.
  • Robustness means giving the dog enough margin that ordinary mistakes and adversity do not cost disproportionate wellbeing.
  • Each generation should be “better” in functional/lived-experience terms, not more exaggerated, fashionable, driven or compliant.
  • No perfection checklist: strengths and difficulties are often two sides of the same trait.

Source material to mine when writing: Doing right by the dog, Embracing your imperfectly perfect dog, Reframing ADHD in dogs: a compassionate perspective, Form follows function: the importance of structure in dog health and performance, The anatomy of a breed standard.
