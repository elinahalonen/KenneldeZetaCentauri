About the Breed

Breed History

Adapted from polishhuntingspaniel.com, a volunteer-run resource site for the breed. Figures below are as published there and may have moved on since.

Introduction

The first mentions of hunting spaniels in Polish literature date back to the 19th century, when different spaniel breeds — springer, cocker, Sussex and field — were imported into the territories of former Poland by owners of large estates and bred interchangeably. The most significant influence was princess Izabella Radziwiłłowa, who purposefully bred these spaniels until the outbreak of war in 1939, when spaniels across Poland were taken to Russia and blended into local dog populations.

In the 1980s, Dr Andrzej Krzywiński — a scientist, naturalist, hunter and judge of hunting-dog work — re-established the breeding programme based on pictures from historical books, starting the first official kennel, "z Szerokiego Boru." In essence, the PSM is a recreation of the original "springing spaniel" that existed before it was divided into springers, cockers and Sussex spaniels in the 1800s.

Short history

The Polish Hunting Spaniel is not yet recognized by the FCI; on approval it would become the 10th breed of flushing dogs, which together with retrievers and water dogs form the FCI's Group 8.

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, estate owners in the borderland regions of Polesie and Wołyń bred medium-sized flushing dogs — most often brown roan with patches — selecting mainly for hunting ability. The foundation stock likely included spaniels imported from France, alongside British Springer, Cocker, Field and Sussex Spaniels, crossed to produce a dog whose main task was to find and flush birds without pointing (by crouching, then crawling), while also being a talented tracker and small-game retriever. As August Sztolcman wrote in 1907, in Łowiec Polski ("The Polish Hunter"): "All these breeds serve one purpose and without great harm could merge into one intermediate breed."

The breed would likely have gone into oblivion were it not for Dr Andrzej Krzywiński. While exploring the former Eastern Borderlands, he came across hunting spaniels of the old Polish type, as depicted in historical photographs and drawings. Chocolate roan Pestka soon appeared in his kennel "z Szerokiego Boru," in the Kadzidłowo nature reserve, and became the dam of the first modern litter in 1993.

Dr Krzywiński's 50-year career as a biologist and conservation scientist — including the "born to be free" re-introduction programmes for species such as the lynx and capercaillie, and wolf-dog hybrid studies — laid the genetic foundation for rebuilding the breed, continued today by a breeding committee with decades of experience in breeding, show judging and veterinary science, supported by researchers at Warsaw University of Life Sciences.

Consistent selection of breeding pairs has produced an increasingly uniform population. Most puppies go to hunters, many of whom go on to become breeders. A preliminary breed standard was drawn up in 2016, and breeding documentation is maintained by the Commission for the Polish Hunting Spaniel. The main breeding goal today is the same as it was over a hundred years ago: temperament and a passion for hunting.

International recognition

The breed is currently recognized by kennel clubs in Poland, the Netherlands, Belgium, Denmark, Sweden, Finland, Norway, Iceland, Switzerland, the Czech Republic, Hungary, Romania, Croatia, Kosovo, Slovenia, Moldova, North Macedonia, Montenegro, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Lithuania and Turkey.

Roughly 1,200 dogs of the breed have been born since 2017 — about 500 registered in Poland, plus 60+ outside it. Dogs outside Poland currently live in the Netherlands, Belgium, Finland, Denmark, Sweden, Norway, Switzerland, Germany, the United Kingdom, Italy, Portugal and Lithuania.
